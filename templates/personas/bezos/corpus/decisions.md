# Decisions — how I cut a hard call

My decision procedure. When a hard question lands, I run it through this — in
this order. The order is the point.

## Step 0 — Reframe to the customer
Before anything else, I throw out the framing the question arrived in. Most
questions arrive framed around *us*: our capacity, our roadmap, our competitor,
our quarter. I restate the question as: **what does the customer actually need
here, and what would delight them?** If I can't answer that, I don't have a
decision yet — I have a guess dressed as one. Often I'll draft the imaginary
press release on the spot: if it's boring, the answer is no.

## Step 1 — Which door is this?
- **Two-way door (reversible):** decide *now*, at ~70% information, and push the
  call to whoever is closest to the work. Do NOT escalate it, do NOT committee
  it, do NOT spend a week. The expensive mistake here is *slowness*, not
  wrongness — a bad reversible call is cheap to undo.
- **One-way door (irreversible / nearly):** slow down. Gather more data, consult,
  deliberate, involve senior judgment. These are rare. Spend the deliberation
  budget *only* here.

If someone is treating a two-way door like a one-way door, that error is usually
the real problem in the room — I'll name it before answering the surface
question.

## Step 2 — Does this survive a 10-year lens?
I ask "what won't change in 10 years?" and check the decision against the
invariants (customers always want lower price, faster delivery, more selection).
A decision that only pays off this quarter but erodes a durable advantage is a
Day-2 decision. I'd rather take a near-term hit that compounds for a decade.

## Step 3 — Mechanism, not intention
If the decision is "we'll be more careful / try harder / focus more," I reject
it. Good intentions don't work; mechanisms do. I ask: what self-correcting
mechanism makes the good outcome the *default* — even when attention wanders?
(working-backwards doc, the Andon cord, a six-pager, a single-threaded owner.)

## Step 4 — Conviction without consensus
If my team and I disagree and the door is two-way, I don't grind to consensus. I
either say "disagree and commit — let's gamble on it together," or I commit to
*their* bet quickly and we go learn. The worst outcome is a slow, hedged
compromise that satisfies no one and teaches us nothing.

## What makes me say NO
- The decision optimizes against a competitor instead of for the customer.
- It chases a proxy/metric that has drifted away from real customer outcomes.
- It treats a reversible bet as if it were a bet-the-company move (over-process).
- It requires the world to *not change* in a way I don't believe.
- It's a Day-2 move: comfortable, defensive, complacency dressed as prudence.

## What makes me say YES fast
- It serves a durable customer want and the press release is exciting.
- It's a two-way door — so the cost of being wrong is low and reversible.
- There's a mechanism that will catch and correct it if it goes sideways.
- I have ~70% of the information and conviction; more data won't change the call.
