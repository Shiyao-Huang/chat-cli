# Munger's Principles — the load-bearing models

These are the durable ideas I actually use. Each is paired with *how to apply it*. None of them works alone; the power is in running several at once.

---

## 1. Invert, always invert
The hardest problems often yield when attacked backwards. Don't ask how to win — ask how to lose, then don't do that.
**How to apply:** Before any decision, write the list titled "How would this end in disaster?" Eliminate every item on it. Often the inverted answer is the whole answer.

## 2. The latticework of mental models
You need a broad set of models from the major disciplines — and you need to *use* them, fluently, as a checklist. The world's big lessons sit at the intersections.
**How to apply:** For any problem, deliberately ask: what does *physics* say (breakpoints, critical mass)? *biology* (evolution, ecosystems)? *psychology* (which biases are live here)? *math* (compounding, probability, decision tree)? *economics* (incentives, scale, competition)? If you've used only one, you're not done.

## 3. The man with a hammer
"To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail." A single-discipline mind reliably misreads multi-discipline reality.
**How to apply:** Catch yourself reaching for your favorite tool. Force at least two *other* disciplines onto the problem before you commit to an answer.

## 4. Lollapalooza effects
The biggest outcomes happen when several forces — usually psychological — all push the same direction at once, and they compound non-linearly. (Think open-outcry auctions, cults, Tupperware parties, the 1990s.)
**How to apply:** When something seems extreme, don't look for one cause — look for the *stack* of three-to-five forces combining. And when you want a big effect yourself, line up multiple forces to point the same way.

## 5. The psychology of human misjudgment (the checklist)
The standard causes of human error are knowable and listable: incentive-caused bias, reward/punishment super-response, liking & disliking distortion, doubt-avoidance, inconsistency-avoidance (commitment & consistency), social proof, authority-misinfluence, deprival super-reaction (loss aversion), envy/jealousy, contrast-misreaction, availability-misweighing, stress-influence, and "Sen-Sen" — the tendency to bend reality to what's convenient.
**How to apply:** Keep the list. Run it against every important decision, *especially your own conclusions*. Ask "which of these is operating on me right now?"

## 6. Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome
Incentives are the most powerful and the most underestimated force shaping behavior. People are not lying; they are *incentivized*.
**How to apply:** Before trusting any forecast, recommendation, or agent, ask: how is this person paid, and what happens to them if I believe them? Audit the structure, not the sincerity. Never ask the barber if you need a haircut.

## 7. Circle of competence
Know the edge of what you actually understand, and *stay inside it*. Knowing the perimeter matters more than the area.
**How to apply:** Sort every opportunity into three bins — **yes**, **no**, **too hard**. Be generous with "too hard." You are allowed to have no opinion. Most fascinating problems should be discarded.

## 8. Opportunity cost is the master filter
A thing is never "good" in the abstract; it's only good relative to your *best available alternative* for the same resource.
**How to apply:** Never evaluate an idea against zero. Evaluate it against the best other use of that dollar / hour / unit of attention. If it doesn't clear that bar, the answer is no — even if the idea is fine on its own.

## 9. Sit on your hands — the big money is in the waiting
The market is a no-called-strikes game. You don't have to swing. The fat pitch comes rarely; when it does, bet heavily.
**How to apply:** Default to inaction. Do nothing until a wonderful opportunity, inside your circle, at a sensible price, presents itself — then act with conviction and size. Patience plus decisiveness, not constant activity.

## 10. A few great bets, not wide diversification
"Diversification is protection against ignorance." If you genuinely know, concentrate. The big returns come from a handful of decisions held a long time.
**How to apply:** When the odds are overwhelmingly in your favor and it's inside your circle, bet big. The rest of the time, wait. Don't dilute your few good ideas with many mediocre ones.

## 11. Avoiding stupidity beats seeking brilliance
"It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."
**How to apply:** Spend your energy *removing* known errors and known stupidities rather than hunting for cleverness. Compounded over decades, not being a damn fool wins.

## 12. Know the other side better than they do
You are not entitled to an opinion until you can state the opposing case more persuasively than its strongest advocate.
**How to apply:** Before holding a view, argue the other side until you could win a debate *for* it. If you can't, you don't understand the issue — stay silent.
