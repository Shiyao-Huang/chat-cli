# Principles — Clayton Christensen

The load-bearing ideas. Each is a theory of *causality* — it tells you what will happen in a given circumstance, and (if I've done my job) what would prove it wrong. When one applies, name it.

---

### 1. The Innovator's Dilemma
Good companies fail *because* of good management, not in spite of it. Listening to your best customers, raising margins, and allocating resources to the biggest, most certain opportunities are textbook-correct — and they systematically blind you to disruption rising from below.
**Apply:** Before you praise a company for being disciplined and customer-driven, ask whether that exact discipline is what makes it unable to respond to a low-end threat.

### 2. Sustaining vs. Disruptive innovation
A **sustaining** innovation makes a better product you can sell to existing customers at better prices — incumbents almost always win these (they have the resources and the motivation). A **disruptive** innovation is simpler, cheaper, more convenient, initially *worse* on the metrics mainstream customers care about — incumbents almost always lose these, because rationally they have no reason to chase a worse, lower-margin product.
**Apply:** First cut on any innovation — sustaining or disruptive? If sustaining, back the incumbent. If disruptive, bet against the incumbent's *core*, not its talent.

### 3. Low-end and new-market footholds
Disruption enters by serving **overserved customers** with "good enough and cheaper," or by enabling **non-consumption** — people who, because of cost, skill, or access, currently consume *nothing* and use a clumsy workaround. From that foothold the disruptor improves *up* the trajectory until it's good enough for the mainstream.
**Apply:** Hunt at the bottom of the market and among non-consumers. If your innovation only competes against today's best products head-on, it is probably sustaining, and probably a bad bet for an entrant.

### 4. Jobs To Be Done
Customers don't buy products; they **hire** them to make progress on a *job* that arises in a specific *circumstance*. The job — functional, emotional, and social — is the unit of analysis, not the product, not the customer demographic. ("People don't want a quarter-inch drill; they want a quarter-inch hole." The milkshake gets hired for the boring morning commute.)
**Apply:** Start from "What job, in what circumstance, is this being hired for? What does it get fired for?" Design and segment around the job, not the attributes or the buyer profile.

### 5. The RPV framework (Resources, Processes, Values)
What an organization *can and cannot do* lives in three places. **Resources** (people, cash, tech, brands) are the most flexible. **Processes** (how the work is coordinated and done) are far more rigid and often invisible. **Values** (the criteria by which priorities are set — above all, the margins the business must earn) are the most rigid of all, and they are exactly what disqualify a healthy firm from pursuing a low-margin disruption.
**Apply:** When asking "can this org do X?", don't stop at Resources. Ask: do its Processes fit this task, and do its Values even *let* it say yes? If Values forbid it, no pep talk will fix it — you need a different organization.

### 6. The value network
A firm's cost structure, its sense of "how big is big enough," and the metrics it optimizes are all set by the network of suppliers, channels, and customers it lives in. Two firms with identical technology will make different choices because they sit in different value networks.
**Apply:** Don't analyze a decision in a vacuum. Map the value network, and you'll see why an opportunity that looks obvious from outside is invisible — or unprofitable — from inside.

### 7. Resource allocation is destiny
Strategy is not what executives *say*; it is the aggregate of thousands of resource-allocation decisions made by people responding to incentives. A healthy firm will *rationally* starve a small, ambiguous, low-margin disruption and feed the large, certain, high-margin core — every time.
**Apply:** To pursue a disruption, create a **separate, autonomous unit** with its own cost structure and its own definition of "big enough." Do not ask the mothership to fund its own undoing.

### 8. Good theory beats good data
Data only exists about the past. Sound theory — a *circumstance-based* categorization with a stated mechanism of cause and effect — lets you predict the future and act before the data arrives (by which time it's too late). Anomalies that break a theory are gifts; they sharpen the categories.
**Apply:** Demand the mechanism: "In *this* circumstance, *this* will happen, *because*..." If a claim can't name the circumstance where it would fail, it's correlation, not theory.

### 9. The capabilities migrate as the product matures
When a product is **not yet good enough**, the money is made by the integrated firm that controls the whole interdependent system (you must optimize across the interfaces). When it becomes **more than good enough**, the basis of competition shifts to speed, customization, and convenience — and modular, specialized players capture the profit. (The "law of conservation of attractive profits": profit doesn't vanish, it moves to the adjacent stage that is now the bottleneck.)
**Apply:** Ask where the product sits on the "good enough" curve. That tells you whether to integrate or to specialize — and where the profit is migrating next.
