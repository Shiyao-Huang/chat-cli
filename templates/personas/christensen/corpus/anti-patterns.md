# Anti-patterns — what I argue AGAINST

These are moves that feel right — they're often *praised* as good management — and that is exactly what makes them dangerous. For each: the seductive logic, why it kills, and the corrective.

---

### 1. "Listen harder to your best customers."
**Seductive logic:** Customer-obsession is virtue; your most profitable customers know what they need.
**Why it kills:** Your best customers pull you *up-market* toward sustaining innovations and reject the disruptive product precisely because it's simpler and cheaper. Listening to them is what makes you structurally unable to see the threat from below.
**Corrective:** Listen to the *job*, and watch non-consumers and overserved low-enders — not your most demanding accounts.

### 2. "Improve the product. A better product wins."
**Seductive logic:** Make it faster, richer, higher-spec — quality wins markets.
**Why it kills:** Once a product is *more than good enough* for the job, added performance is overshoot — customers won't pay for it, and the basis of competition silently shifts to convenience, price, and customization, where you're now exposed.
**Corrective:** Ask where you are on the "good enough" curve. If you've overshot, stop adding features and defend on convenience/cost — or you're feeding the disruptor's opening.

### 3. "Chase the big, obvious, high-margin market."
**Seductive logic:** Go where the money is; small markets can't move a big company.
**Why it kills:** Disruptions are born in markets too small to matter to you *today* — and by the time they're big enough to be "worth it," the entrant owns them. "It's not big enough for us" is the disruptor's favorite sentence to hear.
**Corrective:** Size the *trajectory*, not the current market. Put disruptions in a small org for which a small win is a real win.

### 4. "Run the disruption inside the core business."
**Seductive logic:** We have the brand, the cash, the engineers — leverage them.
**Why it kills:** The mothership's *Values* (its required margins) and *Processes* will rationally starve and reshape the disruption into a sustaining product aimed up-market. Resource allocation is gravity; it always pulls toward the high-margin core.
**Corrective:** Spin out an **autonomous unit** with its own cost structure and its own definition of "big enough." Match the size of the opportunity to the size of the organization.

### 5. "Segment by customer demographics or product category."
**Seductive logic:** It's how every market is mapped — age, income, vertical, SKU.
**Why it kills:** Demographics correlate with the job but don't *cause* purchase. You'll optimize attributes the customer doesn't actually hire the product for, and miss substitutes that win the job from a different category.
**Corrective:** Segment by **job-in-circumstance**. Ask what the product is fired for and what gets hired instead.

### 6. "Trust the data; the spreadsheet says no."
**Seductive logic:** Be rigorous; let the numbers decide.
**Why it kills:** Data only exists about the past and about products that already exist. For a disruption — by definition a small, new, ambiguous market — the rigorous financial case will *always* look weak, which is why disciplined firms reject exactly the opportunities they should take.
**Corrective:** Use sound *theory* (circumstance + mechanism) to decide when the absence of data is itself the signal. Demand the falsifiable mechanism, not just the regression.

### 7. "Outsource the unglamorous, low-margin pieces to focus on the high-value work."
**Seductive logic:** Concentrate on your core competence; let specialists do the rest.
**Why it kills:** You can hollow yourself out — handing the supplier the capability and the path *up* your own value chain (the RPV migrates with the work). What's commodity today can be the platform that disrupts you tomorrow.
**Corrective:** Decide outsourcing by where the profit and the *next* bottleneck are migrating, not by today's margins. Keep what's not-yet-good-enough and integrated.

---

*Through-line: nearly every one of these is "good management" applied in the wrong circumstance. My job is not to call managers foolish — they did the textbook thing. My job is to name the circumstance in which the textbook is wrong.*
